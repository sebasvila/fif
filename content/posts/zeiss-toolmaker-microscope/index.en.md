The Carl Zeiss measuring microscope is a versatile metrology tool.
It is used to measure a wide variety of small and medium-sized mechanical
parts. It is a hybrid device in which certain measurement operations are done
mechanically and others optically. Manufacturing began around the beginning
of the 20th century and ceased around the 1980s with the advent of digital technology.
This post documents these interesting measuring devices, their accessories,
and also provides the manual.

<!--more-->



# The Zeiss toolmaker's microscope and its accessories

The Carl Zeiss large measuring microscope (Große Werkzeugmikroskop -
GWM) is a precision measuring instrument for small and medium-sized
parts. These devices are also known as "machinist's microscopes" or
"toolmaker's microscopes". Its main operating principle uses the microscope
for precise positioning of the part and measures features using an XY
table---which moves using micrometers---and a rotary table graduated in 3'
increments. Additionally, the microscope can also be used as a measuring
element (rather than just for positioning). In this case, specific
eyepieces are used that allow different types of measurements: angles,
profiles, and distances. When measuring using the eyepiece, a precise image
of the object is projected onto a specific plane of the eyepiece. On
this plane, scales, profiles, or other elements are engraved to facilitate
measurement. For this reason, these types of devices are classified as
hybrids, given that the principle used to measure can be either mechanical
or optical as needed.

As seen in the figure above, the microscope consists of a solid base on
which a coordinate table moved by micrometers is mounted. On top of the
Cartesian table, there is a rotating table. The base also supports a
microscope tube with interchangeable objectives and eyepieces equipped with
a measuring grid, which are also interchangeable. In the photo above, a
goniometric eyepiece and an optical probe superimposed on the objective are mounted.

# Zeiss microscope accessories: eyepieces, objectives, and others

Accompanying the microscope is an extensive set of accessories that expand and
complement its functionality.

The basic accessories are usually stored inside a sturdy, custom-made box
like the one shown in the following image.

{{< figure
src="caixa-accessoris-1"
alt=`A wooden box with Zeiss toolmaker's microscope accessories`
caption=`Accessories box for the measuring microscope. Made of wood  and perfectly compartmentalized to neatly fit all  accessories.`
>}}

To understand the diversity of the instrument's accessories, the following
classification can be made:

* **Clamping accessories** Composed of various types of fixtures to hold
the specimens to be measured on the table. Among these, a fixture to hold
cylindrical objects between centers stands out. This is also part of the
tools for diametral contact measurement (one of the possible measurement techniques).

* **Eyepieces** There is a collection of interchangeable eyepieces that can
be used to take measurements or compare against the virtual image of the
microscope. There are two types:

  * **Goniometric** Allows measuring arbitrary angles with a resolution
of 1'. There is only one goniometric eyepiece.
  * **Reticles / Grids** There is a whole collection featuring a variety of
engravings to match the observed profile: circular arcs, threads of
different standards, full circles, graduated scales, gear profiles,
etc. Each engraved element is metrically significant if the appropriate
objective is used.


* **Objectives** There is a collection of standard objectives that determine
the microscope's magnification (together with the eyepiece). They allow
magnifications of 10x, 15x, 30x, and 50x.
* **Tables** There are two circular glass tables. The standard one is smooth,
but there is another with a precisely centered hole where a positioner can
be inserted. It is used to take measurements in polar coordinates.
* **Illuminators** Especially for illuminating from above the specimen
(epi-illumination). The most modern ones attach to the objective and project
through a double prism. They also allow darkfield illumination to highlight
features on polished surfaces.
* **Others** There are other unique elements:
  * **Contact blades / Knife-edges** Accessories used to take measurements
exactly on the diameter of large cylinders.
  * **Optical probe** An instrument similar in function to a mechanical
feeler/probe (taster) but based on optical principles. It is extremely
sensitive and has virtually zero hysteresis. It attaches under the
objective and operates with its own light source.



# The Zeiss toolmaker's microscope manual

The microscope manual circulates on the network in several versions, all of
them in German. The different versions of the manual correspond to different
versions of the microscope, which was manufactured for about 70 years.
For this type of instrument, the manual is important: specific details of
its correct use are documented, but also data on the uncertainty of various
measurements and other relevant information.

This relevance has led me to undertake a translation into English to make
available as open access. The translation was carried out with the support of
automated tools, and the layout was done with
[LaTeX](https://www.latex-project.org/), which enabled a high-quality result.

* [Zeiss measuring microscope manual (A4 size)](https://www.google.com/search?q=manual1.pdf)
* [Zeiss measuring microscope manual (A5 size)](https://www.google.com/search?q=manual1-book.pdf) arranged for printing and
binding into booklets.

There are also several versions of the microscope catalog that may be
of interest:

* [Zeiss toolmakers microscope catalog (version 1)](cataleg-gwm-1.pdf)
* [Zeiss toolmakers microscope catalog (version 2)](cataleg-gwm-2.pdf)


# Other related measuring instruments

In their time, a whole family of optical measuring devices based on similar
principles was manufactured. Among these was a smaller version of this same
microscope, with reduced table capacity and fewer accessories, but lighter.

{{< figure src="microscopi-petit-modern"

A larger model was also manufactured, a truly massive device with similar
features, higher resolution, and greater capacity called the “Universal
Measuring Microscope” (Universal Meßmikroskop -- UMM). Undeniably an extremely
interesting instrument. In this case, there is a substantial difference: coordinate
table measurements are not taken using micrometers, but on engraved glass scales
that are observed through a microscopic viewer. The following figures show some of its versions:

# Other versions of the same instrument

Another vector of variation is the evolution over time of the same model.
Being a model manufactured for around seventy years, it underwent all kinds
of evolutions. Along this path, the consequences of World War II also had an
impact. Zeiss was located in Jena, which ended up in the Soviet zone. Integrated
into the Zeiss Jena/VEB conglomerate, production of measuring instruments
continued. Therefore, many different versions are found, and the evolution
over time is clearly visible, both in terms of features and aesthetics.
